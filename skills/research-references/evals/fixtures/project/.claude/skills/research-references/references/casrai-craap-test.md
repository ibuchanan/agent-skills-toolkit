# The CRAAP Test and Evaluating Source Credibility in Research

The CRAAP test (Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose) is a widely
taught, first-pass checklist for source credibility. It was developed by Sarah
Blakeslee at California State University, Chico, in 2004 for first-year
information-literacy instruction. It works well as a quick screen, but it was
not designed for the problems that arise in scholarly research.

## Five criteria, expanded for scholarly sources

| Criterion | Plain question | Scholarly extra checks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Currency | When was it published or updated? | Has the finding been superseded, replicated, or revised? |
| Relevance | Does it address the research question at the right level? | Is this the primary source, or a secondary summary? Beware citation-chain decay. |
| Authority | Who wrote it and why are they credible? | Institutional affiliation, peer-review type, journal indexing, domain-specific expertise. |
| Accuracy | Is the information correct and verifiable? | Methods/data support the conclusions? Data/code available? Retracted or corrected? |
| Purpose | Why was it created? | Conflict of interest, funding, venue business model, advocacy. |

## Scholarly-specific checks the basic CRAAP list usually skips

- **Journal indexing**: check DOAJ for open-access journals, Scopus, or Web of Science Master Journal List.
- **Predatory/hijacked journals**: verify the editorial board independently, check for aggressive solicitation, undisclosed fees, implausibly fast review.
- **Retractions and post-publication concerns**: check Retraction Watch and PubPeer.
- **Conflict of interest and funding statements**: read them; absence in a venue that should require them is a signal.
- **Data and code availability**: presence is a strong proxy for verifiability.

## The checklist limit: lateral reading and SIFT

Information-literacy research shows that in-page checklist evaluation is
measurably weaker than **lateral reading** — opening new tabs to check what
independent sources say about the source, author, and publisher. Professional
fact-checkers use this reflexively.

SIFT is a shorter, action-oriented lateral-reading method:

1. **Stop** — pause before trusting or sharing.
2. **Investigate the source** — who is behind it and what is their track record?
3. **Find better coverage** — what do more authoritative sources say about the claim?
4. **Trace claims to the original context** — follow secondary reporting back to the primary source.

## AI-generated and AI-surfaced citations

Generative AI can produce plausible-looking citations that do not exist or that
misattribute real claims. The practical rule is unconditional: every citation an
AI tool surfaces must be independently verified at the original source before
use. Locate the actual paper, confirm it says what the AI claimed, and run it
through the same evaluation.

## Practical checklist for the skill

- Apply Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose.
- Search independently for the author, publisher, and journal.
- Check indexing, predatory/hijacked-journal warning signs, retractions, and PubPeer.
- Read conflict-of-interest and funding disclosures.
- Check data and code availability.
- If the source came from an AI tool, verify the citation at the original source.
- If citing a secondary summary, either locate the original or flag that it has not been verified firsthand.
- When two credible sources disagree, check whether they answer the same question, weigh design over recency, and report unresolved disagreement honestly.
